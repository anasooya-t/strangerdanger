Stranger Danger

How Random Video Chatting Websites Impact Children’s Online Safety

Stranger: hello?

Doomscrolling on Tik Tok was already starting to numb her senses. As her boredom hit a new peak, Adeen Asad Mirza, 18, started looking for a fresh outlet to distract herself. That’s when she started talking to strangers online.

Born in the UAE, Mirza started talking to online strangers when she was 10-years-old. First introduced to this space by her friends, she got on a website that had no age verification system that allowed video calls and text chats with virtual strangers from across the world.

“Tons of weird experiences. I can write you an essay on the shit I have seen,” said Mirza as she talked through umingle.com.

Known under different names, these roulette-styled chatting platforms allowed users to talk to strangers anonymously without making an account, which made it easy to start a conversation with anyone, including children.

It’s precursor, Omegle.com, was one of the most popular sites in this space until it shut down in 2023. The website closed down following numerous lawsuits that showed how they were used by online predators to sexually exploit children.

Since then, many websites like Omegle have opened up in this space, including Umingle. Multiple users said they found Umingle while searching for alternatives or were introduced through social media.

“People think that they can say whatever they want when they get online,” said @mallu_potato on Instagram, a 28-year-old content creator.

He appears on screen as a potato masking his face using a camera filter. He holds spontaneous conversations and has gained popularity within adults and children alike.

“Do not point your camera toward screens...Your camera must remain focused on your face at all times.”

But content creators like @mallu_potato have easily been able to bypass these regulations which highlights how ineffective they are.

According to Umingle the website retains IP addresses of the users to enforce bans and prevent people that violate the rules. But a quick search on YouTube gives a list of results on different methods that users can do to get unbanned for free.

Among other interactions, these websites have been historically known for fostering a space for sexual conversations.

A study conducted at Middlesex University in 2024 revealed that only 2% of adult users expressed regret for interacting with an underage person, while 89% blamed the child.

As Mirza shared her experiences, she said how her exposure to sexually explicit conversations desensitized her from sending nudes photos to people on Snapchat as a child.

“I was a minor and they were not,” said Mirza.

Child protection agencies and Facebook parent groups across the world are raising alarms about this internet rabbit hole. But identifying online harm against children has become increasingly difficult.

“We have a list of twenty or even thirty alternatives who use the same basic system,[where] predatory behaviour is a common sight,” said Niels Van Paemel.

Omegle.com developed in 2009 by Leif K. Brooks used to get up to 66 million monthly visits. Along with similar websites like Chatroulette, it became a cultural moment as influencers posted their interactions online.

“Omegle was meant to be a way of strolling down a street in that village, striking up conversations with the people you ran into,” Brooks wrote.

The National Center for Missing and Exploited Children received 6,08,601 reports against Omegle in 2022 alone. The website finally shut down after a case where an 11-year-old was connected with a man who groomed the child for three years.

“Hydra effect: closing a popular webcam platform like Omegle leads to the problem becoming more diffuse,” said Paul Bleakley.

Youngsters like Mirza share how easy it is to find people flashing themselves, masturbating or saying racist and sexist slurs.

According to Naomi Leeds, a major legal difficulty was that platforms did not hold liability until a large enough harm took place.

In the US, COPPA is the baseline privacy law. It required websites aimed at children under 13 to obtain verifiable parental consent.

Harvard Law Review (2026): Social media companies compliance with COPPA “stretches credulity” and it’s clear the system is left to self policing.

“I don’t think COPPA ought to be the principal safeguard for kids,” said Samuel Levine.

While COPPA defines a child as below 13, other states use different age bars—Florida and Tennessee use 18, while Georgia and Louisiana use 16.

“COPPA contributes to protecting children from online sexual abuse but that’s not really its aim,” Levine said.

The law is undergoing changes under COPPA 2.0 legislation which will increase the age bar to sixteen.

From Australia banning social media for under-sixteens to the UK's Online Safety Act, countries are building stronger regulatory frameworks.

“It’s way worse than letting them out on the streets. It’s like you give the whole world access to them,” said Paresh Patel.

But as regulations continue, random chatting websites are hiding in plain sight. Some adult users blame the children and parents—while experts say more accountability needs to be placed on websites.

Online platforms continue to exist in a framework that fails to watch over them, leading to a game of passing the ball where the child’s online safety is on the line.

“There is a 100% chance that children are going to be groomed or at least exposed with live nudity,” said Paemel.
